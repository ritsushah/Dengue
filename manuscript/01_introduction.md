## STRUCTURED ABSTRACT

**Background:**
Dengue virus (DENV) infection is the most rapidly expanding mosquito-borne viral disease globally, with over 14.4 million cases reported to the World Health Organization in 2024. Early detection of the non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen during the acute febrile phase (days 1–7) is critical for timely clinical management, yet the optimal diagnostic platform remains debated. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) offers high accuracy but requires laboratory infrastructure and prolonged turnaround times (4–8 hours per batch), limiting its utility at the point of care. Fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) has emerged as a quantitative, reader-based alternative providing results within 15 minutes; however, its diagnostic performance relative to ELISA has not been systematically synthesized.

**Objectives:**
To determine the pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (PLR/NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of FIA for dengue NS1 antigen detection; to compare FIA diagnostic accuracy directly against ELISA in head-to-head studies; and to explore sources of heterogeneity including infection status, fever duration, DENV serotype, and commercial platform.

**Methods:**
PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched from inception to September 2026 without language restrictions. Studies evaluating FIA for dengue NS1 antigen detection in patients with suspected acute dengue (fever ≤7 days) that reported or permitted reconstruction of 2×2 contingency tables were eligible. Risk of bias was assessed using QUADAS-2. Pooled estimates were calculated using bivariate random-effects models (Reitsma method), and hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) curves were constructed. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I², and between-study variance parameters. Publication bias was evaluated using Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test. Subgroup analyses and bivariate meta-regression were performed for pre-specified covariates. This review was registered with PROSPERO [CRD Number — Pending] and reported according to PRISMA-DTA guidelines.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Global Burden of Dengue: A Public Health Emergency in Evolution

Dengue virus (DENV) infection represents the most significant arthropod-borne viral threat to global public health in the twenty-first century. Caused by four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 through DENV-4) of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus belonging to the family *Flaviviridae*, genus *Flavivirus*, dengue is transmitted primarily by the peridomestic mosquito vectors *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus* [1,2]. The landmark cartographic modeling study by Bhatt et al. (2013) estimated 390 million dengue infections annually (95% credible interval: 284–528 million), of which 96 million (95% CI: 67–136 million) manifest with any level of clinical severity — a figure more than three times the contemporaneous World Health Organization (WHO) estimate [3]. Asia bore 70% of this global burden, with India alone accounting for one-third of all apparent infections [3].

The epidemiological trajectory has since accelerated dramatically. In 2024, the world experienced the highest recorded dengue burden in history, with 14,434,584 cases — including 7,718,585 laboratory-confirmed infections, 52,738 severe cases, and 11,201 deaths — reported across all six WHO regions and more than 100 countries [4]. The Region of the Americas contributed over 90% of this total, with Brazil alone reporting more than 10 million cases and 6,321 deaths [4]. This unprecedented surge reflects the synergistic effects of climate change-driven expansion of *Aedes* vector habitats into previously non-endemic latitudes, unplanned urbanization, population mobility, and waning immunity in populations transitioning between serotype-dominant cycles [5,6]. The WHO has classified dengue among its top ten global public health threats, underscoring the urgency of scalable surveillance and diagnostic capacity [5].

### 1.2 The Diagnostic Imperative: NS1 Antigen as a Biomarker for Acute Dengue

Accurate and timely laboratory diagnosis is the cornerstone of effective dengue management. The clinical presentation of dengue — acute undifferentiated fever, myalgia, retro-orbital pain, and rash — overlaps substantially with that of malaria, chikungunya, Zika virus infection, leptospirosis, typhoid fever, and other acute febrile illnesses prevalent in tropical regions [7,8]. This clinical non-specificity renders syndromic diagnosis unreliable and mandates laboratory confirmation for individual patient management, outbreak detection, and epidemiological surveillance [9].

The non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen has emerged as a pivotal biomarker for early dengue diagnosis. NS1 is a 46–50 kDa highly conserved glycoprotein produced by all four DENV serotypes, secreted from infected host cells into the bloodstream at concentrations reaching up to 50 µg/mL during peak viremia [10,11]. Critically, NS1 antigenemia is detectable from the first day of symptom onset — preceding the appearance of IgM antibodies by 2–4 days — and persists through approximately day 9, spanning the clinically actionable acute febrile window [10,12]. In primary infections, NS1 detection rates exceed 80% during the first four days of illness, with peak concentrations occurring between days 1 and 4 [13]. NS1 levels in serum correlate with viremia as measured by RT-PCR, validating its role as a surrogate marker for active viral replication [10,14].

However, NS1-based diagnostics face important biological limitations. In secondary dengue infections — where heterotypic immunity from a prior DENV serotype exposure primes a rapid anamnestic anti-NS1 IgG response — circulating free NS1 is sequestered into immune complexes, shortening the window of detectability and reducing assay sensitivity to 50–63% during the first four days compared with >80% in primary infections [13,15]. Serotype-dependent variability in NS1 secretion kinetics further complicates diagnostic performance: DENV-1 primary infections produce consistently higher NS1 levels than DENV-2 primary infections, where detection rates may be as low as 50–67% during early illness [13]. These biological constraints establish the imperative for diagnostic platforms that maximize NS1 detection sensitivity across the full spectrum of infection status, serotype, and temporal presentation.

### 1.3 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA): The Laboratory Standard and Its Limitations

The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for dengue NS1 antigen remains the recommended laboratory method for NS1 detection in settings with adequate infrastructure [9,16]. Commercially available platforms — notably the Platelia Dengue NS1 Ag-ELISA (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) and the Panbio Dengue Early ELISA (Abbott Diagnostics) — employ sandwich immunoassay formats with peroxidase-labeled monoclonal antibodies and spectrophotometric readout, providing quantitative results with established cutoff algorithms [17,18].

Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that NS1 ELISA platforms achieve high pooled specificity (97–99%) but variable sensitivity that is profoundly influenced by clinical and virological factors. Da Silva et al. (2014), in a meta-analysis of 30 studies encompassing 12,105 patients, reported pooled sensitivities of 74% (95% CI: 63–82%) for Platelia and 66% (95% CI: 61–71%) for Panbio, with sensitivity declining to 66% and 57%, respectively, in secondary infections [19]. Defined Health et al. (2014), in a multi-country evaluation across six Asian and American sites, confirmed that Platelia sensitivity (66%) exceeded Panbio sensitivity (52%), with both assays showing 100% specificity in non-dengue febrile patients [20]. The most recent large-scale meta-analysis by Pillay et al. (2025), analyzing 161 studies with Bayesian random-effects modeling, estimated NS1 ELISA sensitivity at 90% (95% credible interval: 68–98%) during days 0–4 post-symptom onset, approaching the performance of RT-PCR (95%; 95% CrI: 77–99%) [21].

Despite this diagnostic performance, ELISA has critical operational limitations that constrain its deployment in the clinical settings where dengue diagnostic need is greatest. The assay requires specialized laboratory infrastructure (microplate readers, incubators, washing stations), trained technicians, uninterrupted cold-chain storage (2–8°C), and batch processing of samples, yielding turnaround times of 4–8 hours from the point of sample receipt [16,22,23]. When factoring in specimen transport from primary care or sentinel sites to centralized laboratories — which may add 6–24 hours in rural endemic regions — the total time from clinical presentation to actionable diagnostic result frequently exceeds 24–48 hours [23]. This diagnostic delay is clinically consequential: the critical period for identifying patients at risk of progression to severe dengue (dengue hemorrhagic fever, dengue shock syndrome) falls within days 3–7 of illness, precisely when NS1 levels are declining and the window for supportive intervention is narrowing [2,9]. Batch processing also inherently delays individual patient results during low-volume periods and creates operational bottlenecks during epidemic surges when laboratory capacity is overwhelmed [22].

### 1.4 Fluorescence Immunoassay (FIA): Bridging the Accuracy–Accessibility Gap

Fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) represents a technologically distinct class of rapid diagnostic platforms that seek to combine the objective, reader-based quantification of ELISA with the speed and portability of point-of-care testing. Unlike conventional immunochromatographic tests (ICTs), which rely on subjective visual interpretation of colored lines and are classified as qualitative assays, FIA employs fluorescent-labeled antibodies and a compact optical reader to generate quantitative or semi-quantitative results expressed as a Cutoff Index (COI) value [24,25].

The STANDARD F Dengue NS1 Ag FIA (SD Biosensor Inc., Suwon, South Korea), among the most widely evaluated platforms, uses a disposable test cartridge with a two-dimensional barcode for automated strip recognition by the STANDARD F200 Analyzer. The system provides results in 5–15 minutes from 100 µL of whole blood, serum, or plasma, eliminating the subjectivity of visual interpretation and the inter-operator variability that has been documented to compromise ICT reliability [24,26]. Other FIA-based platforms include the NS1 Ag QUANTI CARD test, which uses a similar fluorescence detection principle [27].

Early clinical evaluations have reported promising diagnostic performance. Pohekar and Bhalchandra (2023), in a prospective study of 300 febrile patients in a tertiary care hospital in India, reported that STANDARD F FIA achieved sensitivity and specificity essentially equivalent to NS1 ELISA, with an observed inverse correlation between COI values and platelet counts in dengue-positive patients, suggesting prognostic utility beyond binary diagnosis [28]. Ghogre et al. (2021) evaluated the NS1 Ag QUANTI CARD FIA against ELISA in 85 clinically suspected dengue specimens and found FIA sensitivity and specificity of 96.5% and 96.4%, respectively — substantially superior to conventional ICT (65.5% sensitivity, 83.9% specificity) — with an almost perfect level of agreement between FIA and ELISA (Cohen's kappa approaching 1.0) [27]. Chaiyo et al. (2022), in a comparative study using a combinatorial RT-PCR and ELISA IgM/IgG reference standard, reported STANDARD F FIA NS1 sensitivity of 79.1% and specificity of 92.3%, slightly exceeding the performance of paired ICT (76.6% sensitivity, 92.3% specificity) [29].

### 1.5 The Evidence Gap: Heterogeneity, Fragmentation, and the Absence of Synthesis

Despite the growing body of individual studies evaluating FIA for dengue NS1 detection, significant uncertainty persists regarding its true diagnostic accuracy relative to ELISA and across diverse clinical and epidemiological contexts. Individual study estimates of FIA sensitivity range from 79% to 97%, and specificity from 92% to 96%, reflecting variability attributable to differences in study populations (primary vs. secondary infection prevalence), circulating serotypes, timing of specimen collection, reference standard used (ELISA alone vs. RT-PCR), geographic setting, and commercial platform evaluated [27–29].

Several meta-analyses have addressed the broader landscape of dengue rapid diagnostic testing, but none has specifically isolated FIA as a distinct technological category. Mata et al. (2020), registered in PROSPERO (CRD42014009885), evaluated ICT-based rapid tests for dengue across 57 studies (40 in meta-analysis), reporting pooled NS1/IgM/IgG ICT sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 96%, but did not disaggregate FIA from visually read ICT platforms [30]. Haider et al. (2022) assessed immunochromatographic NS1 and IgM tests across 15 studies (4,135 participants), finding pooled NS1-only ICT sensitivity of 70.97% and specificity of 94.73% with a DOR of 43.95, but did not separately evaluate FIA-based technologies [31]. Frazer and Norton (2024), in a narrative review of dengue laboratory diagnostics in the vaccine era, discussed rapid platforms including NS1 point-of-care assays but did not provide a formal FIA-specific meta-analysis [32].

This constitutes a critical evidence gap. FIA and conventional visual ICT differ fundamentally in their signal detection mechanism (fluorescence-based objective readout vs. colloidal gold/carbon visual interpretation), analytical sensitivity at the antigen detection limit, quantitative capability (COI values vs. binary positive/negative), and inter-operator reproducibility. Grouping FIA with visually-read ICTs in meta-analytic pooling conflates technologically distinct platforms, obscuring the true diagnostic potential of FIA and providing clinicians, laboratorians, and policymakers with imprecise guidance.

### 1.6 Hypotheses and Objectives

#### 1.6.1 Study Hypotheses

Based on the biological rationale of fluorescence-based signal amplification and the elimination of subjective visual interpretation, we hypothesize that:

**Primary hypothesis:** FIA for dengue NS1 antigen detection demonstrates diagnostic accuracy (pooled sensitivity and specificity) that is non-inferior to — and comparable with — ELISA, while offering substantially shorter turnaround times suitable for point-of-care deployment.

**Secondary hypotheses:**
1. FIA sensitivity is higher in primary dengue infections compared with secondary infections, reflecting the biological kinetics of NS1 antigenemia and anti-NS1 immune complex formation.
2. FIA sensitivity is higher when specimens are collected during the early acute phase (days 0–3 post-fever onset) compared with the late acute phase (days 4–7), consistent with declining NS1 concentrations.
3. FIA diagnostic accuracy varies by DENV serotype, with DENV-1 and DENV-3 yielding higher sensitivity than DENV-2 and DENV-4.
4. The choice of reference standard (RT-PCR vs. ELISA vs. composite) significantly influences the magnitude of pooled FIA accuracy estimates.

#### 1.6.2 Objectives

**Primary objective:**
To determine the pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) for the detection of dengue virus NS1 antigen in patients with suspected acute dengue infection, through a systematic review and bivariate random-effects meta-analysis.

**Secondary objectives:**
1. To compare the diagnostic accuracy of FIA versus ELISA for dengue NS1 detection in a head-to-head analysis, restricted to studies evaluating both tests on the same clinical specimens.
2. To construct a hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) curve and estimate the area under the curve (AUC) for FIA for dengue NS1 detection.
3. To assess between-study heterogeneity and investigate its sources through pre-specified subgroup analyses and bivariate meta-regression for the following covariates: infection status (primary vs. secondary), timing of specimen collection (days 0–3 vs. 4–7), DENV serotype, reference standard type, FIA commercial platform, and geographic region.
4. To evaluate the risk of bias in included studies using the QUADAS-2 tool and to assess the impact of study quality on pooled estimates through sensitivity analysis.
5. To evaluate potential publication bias using Deeks' funnel plot asymmetry test.
6. To generate evidence-based recommendations regarding the diagnostic role of FIA in dengue NS1 detection algorithms, particularly in resource-limited and point-of-care settings.
