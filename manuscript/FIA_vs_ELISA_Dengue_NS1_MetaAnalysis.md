## Abstract

**Background.** Dengue virus (DENV) infection is the most rapidly expanding mosquito-borne viral disease globally, with more than 14.4 million cases reported to the World Health Organization in 2024. Early detection of non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen during the acute febrile phase (days 1–7) is critical for timely care. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is accurate but requires laboratory infrastructure and 4–8 hours per batch. Fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) provides reader-based, quantitative or semi-quantitative results within 15 minutes; its accuracy relative to ELISA has not been synthesized as a distinct technology class.

**Objectives.** To estimate pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (PLR, NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of FIA for dengue NS1; to compare FIA with ELISA on paired specimens; and to explore heterogeneity by infection status, fever duration, serotype, and platform.

**Methods.** PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched from inception to September 2026 without language restrictions. Eligible studies enrolled patients with suspected acute dengue (fever ≤7 days) and reported or permitted reconstruction of 2×2 tables. Risk of bias was assessed with a dengue-tailored QUADAS-2 tool. Pooled estimates used the bivariate random-effects model of Reitsma (equivalent, without covariates, to the hierarchical summary ROC [HSROC] model of Rutter and Gatsonis). Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran’s Q, I², and bivariate τ². Publication bias was assessed with Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test. Reporting followed PRISMA-DTA.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Global burden of dengue

Dengue virus (DENV) infection is among the most important arthropod-borne viral threats of the twenty-first century. Four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1–4) of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA flavivirus are transmitted mainly by *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus* [1,2]. Bhatt et al. estimated 390 million infections per year (95% credible interval 284–528 million), of which 96 million (67–136 million) are apparent at any severity—more than three times contemporaneous WHO figures [3]. Asia bore 70% of that burden; India alone accounted for about one-third of apparent infections [3].

In 2024 the recorded burden reached a historic high: 14,434,584 cases, including 7,718,585 laboratory-confirmed infections, 52,738 severe cases, and 11,201 deaths, across all six WHO regions and more than 100 countries [4]. The Region of the Americas contributed over 90% of reports; Brazil alone exceeded 10 million cases and 6,321 deaths [4]. Climate-driven expansion of *Aedes* habitats, urbanization, mobility, and shifting serotype immunity underlie this surge [5,6]. WHO lists dengue among its leading global health threats [5].

### 1.2 NS1 as an acute-phase biomarker

Clinical dengue—undifferentiated fever, myalgia, retro-orbital pain, rash—overlaps malaria, chikungunya, Zika, leptospirosis, and typhoid [7,8]. Syndromic diagnosis is therefore unreliable; laboratory confirmation is required for care, outbreak detection, and surveillance [2].

NS1 is a 46–50 kDa conserved glycoprotein secreted at up to ~50 µg/mL during peak viremia [9,10]. Antigenemia is detectable from day 1 of symptoms, 2–4 days before IgM, and often through day 9 [9,11]. In primary infection, detection exceeds 80% in the first four days, with peak levels on days 1–4 [12]. Serum NS1 correlates with RT-PCR viremia [9,13].

In secondary infection, anamnestic anti-NS1 IgG sequesters free NS1 into immune complexes, shortening detectability and reducing sensitivity to about 50–63% in the first four days [12,14]. Serotype also matters: primary DENV-1 typically yields higher NS1 than primary DENV-2 (early detection sometimes 50–67%) [12]. Platforms must therefore perform across immune status, serotype, and day of illness.

### 1.3 ELISA: laboratory standard and operational limits

NS1 ELISA remains the recommended laboratory method where infrastructure exists [2,15]. Platelia Dengue NS1 Ag-ELISA (Bio-Rad) and Panbio Dengue Early ELISA (Abbott) use sandwich formats with peroxidase-labelled monoclonal antibodies [16,17].

Pooled specificity is high (97–99%); sensitivity varies. Da Silva et al. (30 studies, 12,105 patients) reported Platelia sensitivity 74% (63–82%) and Panbio 66% (61–71%), falling to 66% and 57% in secondary infection [18]. Guzmán et al., in a six-country evaluation, found Platelia more sensitive than Pan-E (66% vs 52%) with 100% specificity in non-dengue febrile patients [19]. Pillay et al. (161 studies; Bayesian random-effects without assuming a perfect gold standard) estimated NS1 ELISA sensitivity 90% (95% CrI 68–98%) on days 0–4, approaching RT-PCR (95%; 77–99%) [20].

ELISA nonetheless requires plate readers, incubators, trained staff, 2–8 °C storage, and batching, with 4–8 hours from sample receipt [15,21,22]. Transport from primary or rural sites may add 6–24 hours, so time-to-result often exceeds 24–48 hours [22]. The critical phase of severe dengue clusters around days 3–7, when NS1 is already declining [2,7]. Batch queues also stall individual results in low-volume periods and overwhelm laboratories during surges [21].

### 1.4 Fluorescence immunoassay

FIA aims to combine ELISA-like objective readout with point-of-care speed. Unlike visual immunochromatographic tests (ICTs), FIA uses fluorescent labels and a compact optical reader to report a cutoff index (COI) [23,24]. STANDARD F Dengue NS1 Ag FIA (SD Biosensor) uses a barcoded cartridge on the F200 analyzer and returns results in 5–15 minutes from 100 µL of whole blood, serum, or plasma [23,25]. The NS1 Ag QUANTI CARD uses a related fluorescence principle [26].

Pohekar and Bhalchandra (n = 300, India) found STANDARD F essentially equivalent to NS1 ELISA, with an inverse COI–platelet relationship that is hypothesis-generating for severity [27]. Ghogre reported QUANTI CARD sensitivity 96.5% and specificity 96.4% versus ELISA, versus ICT 65.5%/83.9%, with near-perfect FIA–ELISA agreement [26]. Chaiyo et al., against combinatorial RT-PCR plus IgM/IgG ELISA, reported STANDARD F NS1 sensitivity 79.1% and specificity 92.3%, slightly above paired ICT (76.6%/92.3%) [25].

### 1.5 Evidence gap

Published FIA sensitivity ranges from about 79% to 97% and specificity from 92% to 96%, reflecting mix of primary/secondary infection, serotype, sampling day, reference standard, geography, and brand [25–27]. Prior syntheses did not isolate FIA. Mata et al. (PROSPERO CRD42014009885; 57 studies, 40 in meta-analysis) reported NS1/IgM/IgG ICT sensitivity 91% and specificity 96% without separating FIA from visual ICT [28]. Haider et al. (15 studies, 4,135 participants) found NS1-only ICT sensitivity 70.97% and specificity 94.73% (DOR 43.95) [29]. Frazer and Norton reviewed dengue diagnostics in the vaccine era but did not pool FIA [30].

FIA and visual ICT differ in signal (fluorescence vs colloidal gold), interpretation (COI vs lines), analytical limit, and operator dependence. Pooling them obscures FIA’s true performance and misleads procurement.

### 1.6 Hypotheses and objectives

**Primary hypothesis.** FIA for dengue NS1 is diagnostically comparable to ELISA, with a turnaround time suitable for near-point-of-care use.

**Secondary hypotheses.** (i) Sensitivity is higher in primary than secondary infection. (ii) Sensitivity is higher on days 0–3 than days 4–7. (iii) Accuracy varies by serotype (higher for DENV-1/3 than DENV-2/4). (iv) Reference-standard choice (RT-PCR vs ELISA vs composite) changes pooled estimates.

**Primary objective.** To estimate pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR, NLR, and DOR of FIA for dengue NS1 in suspected acute dengue, using a bivariate random-effects meta-analysis.

**Secondary objectives.** (1) Head-to-head FIA vs ELISA on the same specimens. (2) HSROC curve and AUC. (3) Heterogeneity and pre-specified subgroups/meta-regression (infection status, fever day, serotype, reference, platform, region). (4) QUADAS-2 and quality-restricted sensitivity analysis. (5) Deeks’ test. (6) Algorithm recommendations for resource-limited and point-of-care settings.

## 2. Methods

This diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) review was designed and reported according to PRISMA-DTA [31], the PRISMA-DTA explanation and elaboration [32], PRISMA 2020 [33], PRISMA-S [34], and Cochrane DTA guidance [35]. STARD 2015 informed assessment of primary-study reporting [36]. A PRISMA-DTA checklist is Supplementary Table S1. Ethical approval was not required (secondary analysis of published data).

### 2.1 Protocol and registration

The protocol was registered with PROSPERO before formal screening and extraction (CRD[XXXXXXX], pending; version 1.0, August 2026). Deviations will be dated, justified, and reported.

### 2.2 Review question (PIRD)

> What is the diagnostic accuracy of fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) compared with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for detection of dengue virus NS1 antigen in patients with suspected acute dengue infection?

| Component | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Population** | Any age; clinically suspected acute dengue; fever ≤7 days from onset; any healthcare setting in endemic or epidemic regions. Specimens: venous whole blood, serum, or plasma. |
| **Index test** | Commercial FIA for dengue NS1 (e.g., STANDARD F, QUANTI CARD, or equivalent reader-interpreted fluorescence assays). Positivity: manufacturer COI. |
| **Reference standard** | Tier 1: RT-PCR; Tier 2: virus isolation ± RT-PCR; Tier 3: composite (RT-PCR + paired IgM/IgG seroconversion ± NS1 ELISA); Tier 4: NS1 ELISA alone if molecular testing is unavailable. ELISA is also the **comparator** in paired analyses. |
| **Target condition** | Acute DENV-1–4 infection confirmed by NS1 antigenemia and/or virological evidence in the febrile window. |

### 2.3 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** Patients of any age with suspected acute dengue and fever ≤7 days (explicit criterion or documented mean/median ≤7 days). FIA for NS1 with automated or semi-automated COI. Reference: RT-PCR, isolation, composite, and/or NS1 ELISA. Designs: cross-sectional DTA, prospective or retrospective diagnostic cohorts, head-to-head comparisons, nested surveillance. Extractable or reconstructable 2×2 table (true positives [TP], false positives [FP], false negatives [FN], true negatives [TN]). Original data; peer-reviewed articles, data-sufficient abstracts, and preprints (preprints flagged for sensitivity analysis). No language or date limits.

**Exclusion.** Asymptomatic seroprevalence or blood-bank screening only; convalescent-only specimens (>7 days) without paired acute samples; animal or spiked-sample studies without a clinical cohort; saliva/urine only unless paired with a standard matrix; combined NS1/IgM/IgG assays that cannot be disaggregated; exclusively immunocompromised cohorts without a general arm; archived specimens without clinical context; reviews, editorials, case reports/series without denominators; non-reconstructable 2×2 data; duplicate datasets (most complete report retained).

### 2.4 Information sources and search

Searches ran from inception to September 2026 (updated immediately before final analysis) in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection (SCI-EXPANDED, ESCI), Scopus, ScienceDirect (title–abstract–keyword), Cochrane Library (CDSR, CENTRAL), and Google Scholar (first 200 by relevance). Grey sources: ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, ProQuest Dissertations, medRxiv/bioRxiv, WHO IRIS, PAHO guidance, manufacturer IFUs, and backward/forward citation tracking.

The search was validated by retrieval of known eligible reports [20,25–27] and developed with PRESS 2015 principles [37].

### 2.5 Study selection

Records were exported to Zotero or EndNote, deduplicated, and screened in Rayyan or Covidence. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts after a 50-record calibration (proceed when Cohen’s κ ≥ 0.80) [38]. Unclear abstracts advanced to full text. Full-text exclusions used a taxonomy: wrong population; wrong index test; no 2×2 data; wrong target condition; duplicate; other. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, then a third reviewer. Reviewers were not blinded to journal or authors. Counts followed a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

### 2.6 Data collection

A piloted form (Supplementary File S3) was applied independently by two reviewers to every included study. Authors were emailed once plus a 14-day reminder for missing 2×2 data. Missing items were coded NR and not imputed except the pre-specified continuity correction.

Extracted items: study identifiers, design, setting, funding; N, prevalence, age, sex, fever day, WHO 2009 class [2], primary/secondary status and method, serotypes, specimen; FIA brand, reader, COI, IFU cutoff, volume, turnaround, blinding, indeterminates; reference type, ELISA/RT-PCR details, same-specimen testing, interval, differential verification; 2×2 cells and derived accuracy.

When cells were unreported: TP = Se × n_positive; FN = (1 − Se) × n_positive; TN = Sp × n_negative; FP = (1 − Sp) × n_negative (rounded to integers). Non-reconstructable studies were described narratively only.

### 2.7 Risk of bias (QUADAS-2)

QUADAS-2 [39] was tailored to dengue NS1 FIA (Supplementary File S4). Two reviewers assessed studies after a three-study calibration.

**Table 1. Tailored QUADAS-2 signalling questions**

| Domain | Questions (standard and *review-specific*) |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|
| 1. Patient selection | Consecutive or random sample? Case-control avoided? Inappropriate exclusions avoided? *Fever ≤7 days at draw?* |
| 2. Index test (FIA) | Interpreted without knowledge of the reference? Threshold pre-specified? *Performed per IFU? Indeterminates reported and handled (<5% excluded)?* |
| 3. Reference standard | Likely to classify acute dengue correctly? Interpreted without knowledge of FIA? *Incorporation bias avoided? Applied in its diagnostic window?* |
| 4. Flow and timing | Interval appropriate (same specimen or ≤24 h)? Same reference for all? All patients analysed? *Flow described (STARD-style)?* |

Judgments: Low / High / Unclear for risk of bias; Domains 1–3 also for applicability. High RoB examples: two-gate sampling; post-hoc COI optimization; IgM-only reference; differential verification; >5% unexplained exclusions. NS1 ELISA as sole reference was Unclear (imperfect standard), not automatically High. Visualization used `robvis` (`tool = "QUADAS-2"`) [40]. Overall RoB entered sensitivity analysis and meta-regression.

### 2.8 Statistical synthesis

**Outcomes.** Pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR = Se/(1 − Sp), NLR = (1 − Se)/Sp, DOR, and HSROC (AUC, 95% confidence and prediction regions). Secondary: paired FIA vs ELISA, Cohen’s κ, narrative turnaround time.

**Primary model.** Bivariate random-effects GLMM (Reitsma) in R `mada::reitsma()` [41,42], jointly modelling logit sensitivity and logit false-positive rate with unstructured Σ (τ²_Se, τ²_FPR, correlation ρ). Variance: REML (ML in sensitivity analysis). PLR, NLR, and DOR 95% CIs: parametric bootstrap (10,000 draws). Without covariates this model is equivalent to HSROC (Rutter and Gatsonis) [43,44]. Zero cells: +0.5 to all four cells [35,41]; 0.1 and 1.0 in sensitivity analysis.

**Head-to-head.** Separate bivariate models on the overlapping study set when both tests were applied to the same specimens; ≥4 studies required.

**Publication bias.** Deeks’ regression of ln(DOR) on 1/√ESS, where ESS = 4 × n_pos × n_neg / (n_pos + n_neg); two-sided *p* < 0.10 indicated possible small-study effects [45].

**Software.** R ≥ 4.3.0 [46] with `mada` [41], `metafor` [47], `meta`, `mvmeta`, `robvis` [40], `ggplot2`. Script: Supplementary File S5.

### 2.9 Heterogeneity and subgroups

Heterogeneity: forest/HSROC inspection; Cochran’s Q (α = 0.10) [48]; I² (0–40% might not be important; 30–60% moderate; 50–90% substantial; 75–100% considerable) [48]; bivariate τ²; ρ < −0.3 suggesting a threshold effect.

Subgroup bivariate models required ≥4 studies per stratum: primary vs secondary [14]; days 0–3 vs 4–7 [9]; DENV-1–4 [12]; reference tier; STANDARD F vs other FIA; region; pediatric vs adult; prospective vs retrospective. Mixed (unstratified) data stayed in the overall model only.

### 2.10 Sensitivity analyses and meta-regression

Pre-specified: (1) exclude High RoB in ≥2 domains; (2) RT-PCR/isolation reference only; (3) leave-one-out; (4) peer-reviewed full texts only; (5) continuity corrections 0.1 and 1.0; (6) ML vs REML.

Univariable bivariate meta-regression (`cbind(tsens, tfpr) ~ covariate`) for reference type, brand, specimen, design, prevalence (≤50% vs >50%), region, fever category, infection mix, and overall RoB. Nested ML models compared by likelihood-ratio test (*p* < 0.05). GRADE for DTA [49] was applied if the evidence base allowed (Supplementary Table S6).

### 2.11 Patient involvement and data sharing

Patients were not involved in protocol design. Upon acceptance, 2×2 data, QUADAS-2 judgments, R code, and the PRISMA-DTA checklist will be deposited in a public repository.

### 4.2 Clinical and operational implications

WHO 2009 guidance ties risk stratification to defervescence (typically days 3–7) [2]. NS1 peaks in the first four days, often before IgM [9,30]. ELISA plus transport commonly costs 24–48 hours [15,22]—enough to span the critical phase. FIA in 5–15 minutes [23,25,27] changes *when* fluid management and admission can be informed.

In endemic emergency departments the differential includes malaria, leptospirosis, typhoid, chikungunya, and Zika [7,8]. A positive FIA (PLR [x.xx]) can start dengue-protocol monitoring without waiting for ELISA. COI–platelet correlation remains hypothesis-generating [27]. Because NS1 sensitivity falls in secondary infection and after day 3–4 [9,14,17], a negative FIA in a patient with warning signs or prior dengue **must not** stop the work-up; add IgM/IgG or RT-PCR [28,29]. Automated COI reduces visual-line disagreement [17,39] if the manufacturer cutoff is used.

ELISA is still preferable in equipped central laboratories [15,18–20]. FIA needs power (or a charged analyzer) and cartridges: it is **near-POC**, not zero-infrastructure. National tiers should be: visual ICT or FIA at the periphery; ELISA and RT-PCR at the reference laboratory; composite algorithms for severe or discordant cases [2,4,30].

**Proposed algorithm (refine after NLR is known).**  
(1) Days 0–3, reader available: FIA NS1 first-line; positive → dengue pathway; confirm with ELISA/RT-PCR as capacity allows.  
(2) Days 4–7 or suspected secondary infection: FIA plus IgM (± IgG); dual-negative → RT-PCR or paired serology [9,20,29].  
(3) Severe dengue, ICU, or surveillance confirmation: RT-PCR and/or NS1 ELISA [20,30].  
(4) Negative FIA, high clinical probability: never a stop test.

### 4.3 Strengths

The question is technology-specific: Mata and Haider pooled visual ICTs [28,29]. Reporting follows PRISMA-DTA/S and dual independent screening/extraction [31–34]. QUADAS-2 was adapted for fever timing, IFU, indeterminates, incorporation bias, and diagnostic window [36,39]. Pooling used Reitsma/HSROC rather than univariate Se/Sp or DOR-only models [42–44], with Deeks’ test [45] and pre-specified NS1-biology covariates [9,12,14]. The paired contrast avoids case-mix confounding. Analysis code is archived.

### 4.4 Limitations

RT-PCR, isolation, composite algorithms, and NS1 ELISA are not interchangeable. ELISA-only reference can inflate agreement (incorporation bias) [39]; RT-PCR after viremia can lower apparent FIA sensitivity [9,20]. We did not use latent-class models as the primary analysis [20]. Fever day is often reported as a mixed mean, blurring days 0–3 vs 4–7. Serotype-specific 2×2 tables are scarce; DENV-4 and DENV-2 have been associated with lower NS1 ELISA sensitivity [18,19]. Flavivirus cross-reactivity (Zika, occasionally YFV/JEV) can inflate false positives if controls are blood donors rather than other acute flavivirus infections [17,30]. IgM/IgG ratios imperfectly classify immune status. Lot, reader model, and sponsorship were incompletely reported. Expected k is smaller than ICT reviews [28,29], so prediction regions may be wide. Continuity correction is conventional but arbitrary. Language bias may persist despite no language filter. This review did not meta-analyse cost, COI–severity, combined NS1+IgM cartridges (unless NS1 was separable), donor screening, or pre-vaccination IgG [30].

### 4.5 Future research and policy

Needed: prospective STARD-compliant paired FIA/ELISA studies with independent RT-PCR and day-of-illness to the day [36,51]; powered secondary-infection strata; regional flavivirus challenge panels [17,30]; lot and borderline-COI reporting; impact studies (time-to-decision, admission, antibiotics, severe dengue); four-way FIA vs ICT vs ELISA vs RT-PCR; vaccine-era strata in which NS1 may outlast IgM/IgG for acute diagnosis [30].

Policy: do not tender “rapid dengue tests” as one class. Place FIA in near-POC triage; do not use a negative FIA as standalone rule-out. Keep ELISA/RT-PCR for confirmation, serotyping, and EQA of peripheral FIA [2,20]. State in case definitions whether FIA NS1 is confirmatory or screening.

### 4.6 Conclusions

Early NS1 detection in the first week of fever is central to dengue care. ELISA’s accuracy is offset by delay; visual ICT’s speed is offset by modest, operator-dependent sensitivity [17,18,22,28,29]. FIA was built for that gap.

In this PRISMA-DTA review, FIA pooled sensitivity was **[xx.x%]** (95% CI [ll]–[uu]) and specificity **[xx.x%]** ([ll]–[uu]), with DOR **[xxx]** and AUC **[0.xx]**. Versus ELISA on paired specimens, FIA was **[fill from Table 7]**. Performance [was / was not] worse in secondary infection and [was / was not] better in the first three days of illness [9,12,14].

**Actionable points.** (1) Use FIA as a rapid, objective NS1 **rule-in** test in triage, emergency, and outbreak settings when a reader can be maintained—especially days 0–3. (2) Do not use a negative FIA to exclude dengue in secondary infection, late acute presentation, or severe disease; reflex to IgM/IgG, ELISA, and/or RT-PCR. (3) Do not equate FIA with visual ICT or ELISA in guidelines or tenders. (4) Keep ELISA/RT-PCR as the laboratory reference. (5) Commission paired, day-stratified, serotype-aware studies with independent molecular reference before making FIA the sole confirmatory test.

Until that evidence accumulates, FIA is a clinically useful **near-point-of-care NS1 assay** that can shorten time-to-diagnosis toward ELISA-like objectivity without fully replacing laboratory confirmation.
