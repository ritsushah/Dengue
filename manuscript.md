## Abstract

**Background.** Dengue virus (DENV) infection remains a leading mosquito-borne threat. NS1 antigen detection during the first week of fever supports early care. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is accurate but slow. Reader-based fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) returns a cutoff index in about 15 minutes. Prior rapid-test meta-analyses pooled FIA with visually read immunochromatographic tests (ICTs). This review isolates commercial FIA.

**Objectives.** To estimate the diagnostic accuracy of FIA for dengue NS1 antigen in suspected acute dengue, and to compare FIA with NS1 ELISA where paired data existed.

**Methods.** PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and ScienceDirect were searched from inception to 19 August 2026. Cochrane Library and Google Scholar were supplementary. Scopus was not licensed at the search site and was not searched. Eligible studies evaluated a commercial reader-interpreted FIA for dengue NS1 and reported or permitted reconstruction of a 2×2 table versus RT-PCR, a composite laboratory standard, or NS1 ELISA. Risk of bias was assessed with a dengue-tailored QUADAS-2 tool. Sensitivity and specificity were pooled with a bivariate random-effects (Reitsma) model. Reporting followed PRISMA-DTA.

**Results.** Database searches identified 1,889 records (PubMed 423, Embase 959, Web of Science 432, ScienceDirect 75); 807 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,082 unique bibliographic records. Five studies (794 specimens; 320 reference-positive) entered quantitative synthesis. Pooled sensitivity was 86.7% (95% CI 76.0–93.1) and pooled specificity 94.0% (89.6–96.6). The positive likelihood ratio was 14.3 (7.5–26.5) and the negative likelihood ratio 0.14 (0.07–0.26). Heterogeneity was moderate for sensitivity (I² = 51%) and lower for specificity (I² = 35%). In the three STANDARD F evaluations that did not use NS1 ELISA as the sole reference, pooled sensitivity was 80.5% (75.1–85.0) and specificity 91.5% (87.7–94.2). Two QUANTI CARD studies that used NS1 ELISA as the reference reported sensitivity 96.6–100% and specificity 96.4–98.4%; those figures measure concordance with ELISA, not accuracy versus an independent standard. A sixth study (Pohekar 2023, n = 300) could not be pooled: off-diagonal 2×2 cells were not published. Three of five pooled studies were at high overall risk of bias. Dual independent screening of all 1,082 unique records was not performed.

**Conclusions.** Commercial FIA for dengue NS1 has a likelihood ratio profile that supports **rule-in** during the febrile window when the reader is available. An NLR of 0.14 does **not** support standalone rule-out. The evidence base is small, reference standards are mixed, and several studies used two-gate or enriched sampling. FIA should not be procured or guideline-coded as if it were visual ICT or NS1 ELISA. ELISA and RT-PCR remain necessary for confirmation, serotyping, and negative results in high-probability patients.

**Registration.** PROSPERO CRD pending.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Global burden of dengue

Dengue virus (DENV) infection is among the most important arthropod-borne viral threats of the twenty-first century. Four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1–4) of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA flavivirus are transmitted mainly by *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus* [1,2]. Bhatt et al. estimated 390 million infections per year (95% credible interval 284–528 million), of which 96 million (67–136 million) are apparent at any severity [3]. Asia bore about 70% of that burden [3].

In 2024 the recorded burden reached a historic high: 14,434,584 cases, including 7,718,585 laboratory-confirmed infections, 52,738 severe cases, and 11,201 deaths, across all six WHO regions [4]. The Region of the Americas contributed over 90% of reports [4]. Climate-driven expansion of *Aedes* habitats, urbanization, mobility, and shifting serotype immunity underlie this surge [5,6]. WHO lists dengue among its leading global health threats [5].

### 1.2 NS1 as an acute-phase biomarker

Clinical dengue—undifferentiated fever, myalgia, retro-orbital pain, rash—overlaps malaria, chikungunya, Zika, leptospirosis, and typhoid [7,8]. Syndromic diagnosis is unreliable; laboratory confirmation is required for care, outbreak detection, and surveillance [2].

NS1 is a 46–50 kDa conserved glycoprotein secreted at high concentration during peak viremia [9,10]. Antigenemia is detectable from day 1 of symptoms, often 2–4 days before IgM, and frequently through day 9 [9,11]. In primary infection, detection commonly exceeds 80% in the first four days [12]. In secondary infection, anamnestic anti-NS1 IgG sequesters free NS1 into immune complexes, shortening detectability and reducing sensitivity [12,14]. Serotype also matters: primary DENV-1 typically yields higher NS1 than primary DENV-2 [12]. Any platform must therefore perform across immune status, serotype, and day of illness.

### 1.3 ELISA: laboratory standard and operational limits

NS1 ELISA remains the recommended laboratory method where infrastructure exists [2,15]. Platelia Dengue NS1 Ag-ELISA (Bio-Rad) and Panbio Dengue Early ELISA (Abbott) use sandwich formats with peroxidase-labelled monoclonal antibodies [16,17].

Pooled specificity is high (about 97–99%); sensitivity varies. Da Silva et al. (30 studies) reported Platelia sensitivity 74% (63–82%) and Panbio 66% (61–71%), falling further in secondary infection [18]. Guzmán et al., in a six-country evaluation, found Platelia more sensitive than Pan-E (66% vs 52%) with 100% specificity in non-dengue febrile patients [19]. Pillay et al. estimated NS1 ELISA sensitivity 90% (95% CrI 68–98%) on days 0–4 in a Bayesian model that does not assume a perfect gold standard [20].

ELISA nonetheless requires plate readers, incubators, trained staff, cold storage, and batching, with 4–8 hours from sample receipt [15,21,22]. Transport from primary or rural sites may add 6–24 hours, so time-to-result often exceeds 24–48 hours [22]. The critical phase of severe dengue clusters around days 3–7, when NS1 is already declining [2,7].

### 1.4 Fluorescence immunoassay as a distinct technology

FIA aims to combine an objective, reader-based cutoff index (COI) with near-point-of-care speed. Unlike visual ICT, FIA uses fluorescent labels and a compact optical reader [23,24]. STANDARD F Dengue NS1 Ag FIA (SD Biosensor) uses a barcoded cartridge on an analyzer and returns results in 5–15 minutes from whole blood, serum, or plasma [23,25]. The NS1 Ag QUANTI CARD (J. Mitra) uses a related fluorescence principle with an iQuant reader [26,52].

Individual evaluations have reported STANDARD F NS1 sensitivity of 79.1% and specificity of 92.3% against a combinatorial RT-PCR plus antibody ELISA standard [25], and QUANTI CARD sensitivity of 96.5% and specificity of 96.4% against NS1 ELISA [26]. These numbers cannot be compared as if they estimated the same parameter: the reference standards differ.

### 1.5 Evidence gap

Prior syntheses did not isolate FIA. Mata et al. (57 studies, 40 in meta-analysis) reported NS1/IgM/IgG ICT sensitivity 91% and specificity 96% without separating FIA from visual ICT [28]. Haider et al. (15 studies, 4,135 participants) found NS1-only ICT sensitivity 70.97% and specificity 94.73% (DOR 43.95) [29]. Frazer and Norton reviewed dengue diagnostics in the vaccine era but did not pool FIA [30].

FIA and visual ICT differ in signal (fluorescence vs colloidal gold), interpretation (COI vs lines), analytical limit, and operator dependence. Pooling them obscures FIA performance and misleads procurement.

### 1.6 Objectives

**Primary objective.** To estimate pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (PLR, NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of commercial FIA for dengue NS1 in suspected acute dengue, using a bivariate random-effects meta-analysis.

**Secondary objectives.** (1) Head-to-head FIA vs ELISA on the same specimens when an independent reference was available. (2) Description of the SROC in the observed false-positive-rate region. (3) Heterogeneity and pre-specified subgroups (fitted only if k ≥ 4 per stratum). (4) QUADAS-2. (5) Deeks’ test. (6) Placement of FIA in a diagnostic algorithm.

## 2. Methods

This diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) review was designed and reported according to PRISMA-DTA [31], the PRISMA-DTA explanation and elaboration [32], PRISMA 2020 [33], PRISMA-S [34], and Cochrane DTA guidance [35]. STARD 2015 informed assessment of primary-study reporting [36]. Ethical approval was not required (secondary analysis of published data).

### 2.1 Protocol and registration

A protocol was prepared before quantitative synthesis (`protocols/prospero_protocol.md`, version 1.0, August 2026). Submission to PROSPERO has not yet returned a CRD number. Deviations from the written protocol are listed in Section 2.12.

### 2.2 Review question (PIRD)

> What is the diagnostic accuracy of fluorescence immunoassay (FIA) compared with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for detection of dengue virus NS1 antigen in patients with suspected acute dengue infection?

| Component | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Population** | Any age; clinically suspected acute dengue; intended fever window ≤7 days from onset. Specimens: venous whole blood, serum, or plasma. |
| **Index test** | Commercial FIA for dengue NS1 (STANDARD F, QUANTI CARD, or equivalent reader-interpreted fluorescence assays). Positivity: manufacturer COI. |
| **Reference standard** | Tier 1: RT-PCR; Tier 2: virus isolation ± RT-PCR; Tier 3: composite (RT-PCR + serology ± NS1 ELISA); Tier 4: NS1 ELISA alone if molecular testing is unavailable. ELISA is also the **comparator** in paired analyses. |
| **Target condition** | Acute DENV-1–4 infection in the febrile window. |

### 2.3 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** Patients with suspected acute dengue. Commercial FIA for NS1 with automated or semi-automated COI. Reference: RT-PCR, isolation, composite, and/or NS1 ELISA. Designs: cross-sectional DTA, prospective or retrospective diagnostic cohorts, head-to-head comparisons. Extractable or reconstructable 2×2 table (true positives [TP], false positives [FP], false negatives [FN], true negatives [TN]). Original data. No language or date limits.

**Exclusion.** Asymptomatic seroprevalence or blood-bank screening only; convalescent-only specimens without paired acute samples; animal or spiked-sample studies without a clinical cohort; saliva/urine only; combined NS1/IgM/IgG assays that cannot be disaggregated to NS1; reviews, editorials, correspondence, and case reports without denominators; non-reconstructable 2×2 data; duplicate datasets (most complete report retained). Experimental laboratory platforms (for example multilayer host-biomarker fluorescent assays, smartphone research lateral-flow devices, chopped-optical biosensors) were excluded as not equivalent to commercial cartridge FIA. Automated laboratory ELFA (VIDAS NS1) was excluded as a different technology class.

Fever duration ≤7 days was the protocol population. Studies of clinically suspected acute dengue that did not report day of illness were not automatically excluded; they were retained with Unclear applicability and examined in a documented-fever sensitivity analysis. This is a protocol deviation (Section 2.12).

### 2.5 Study selection

Records were harvested as RIS/CSV/PMID lists, parsed to a common schema, and deduplicated by DOI, then PMID, then normalized title plus year, with a title-only fallback (`analysis/deduplicate_records.py`).

**Protocol deviation — screening.** Dual independent title/abstract screening of all unique bibliographic records, calibration to Cohen’s κ ≥ 0.80, and blinded Rayyan/Covidence workflow were specified in the protocol and were **not completed**. Because the electronic strategy used (FIA OR ELISA), the unique set is dominated by ELISA-only NS1 papers. Selection used:

1. Title/field filter of the unique bibliographic set for FIA, fluorescence immunoassay, STANDARD F, QUANTI CARD, and iQuant.
2. Full-text assessment of those records, plus one unique-set paper that named STANDARD F in the full text but not the title (Zapata-de la Cruz 2026) [53].
3. Two protocol-scoping validations not retrieved among the four bibliographic databases: Ghogre 2021 and Pohekar 2017 [26,52].

Inter-rater κ is therefore not reported. Missed FIA papers whose titles lack FIA terms remain possible. This limitation is carried into GRADE.

Full-text exclusions used: wrong population; wrong index test (including experimental platforms and visual ICT); no reconstructable NS1 2×2; correspondence; conference abstract without cells; duplicate.

### 2.6 Data collection

A standardized form (`data/extraction_template.md`) was applied to included reports. Corresponding authors were not successfully used to recover missing 2×2 cells for Pohekar 2023; that study is described narratively only.

When cells were unreported, TP, FN, TN, and FP were reconstructed from published sensitivity, specificity, and denominators. Integer solutions were required to reproduce published Se, Sp, PPV, and NPV to the reported precision. Locked cells are in `data/extraction/included_2x2_2026-08-19.csv`. Missing items were coded not reported and were not imputed except the pre-specified continuity correction at the modelling stage.

### 2.7 Risk of bias (QUADAS-2)

QUADAS-2 [39] was tailored to dengue NS1 FIA (`analysis/quadas2_protocol.md`). Signalling questions added fever duration ≤7 days, IFU adherence, indeterminate handling, incorporation bias (ELISA as both comparator and sole reference), and reference-standard diagnostic window.

Judgments: Low / High / Unclear for risk of bias; Domains 1–3 also for applicability. NS1 ELISA as sole reference was judged Unclear for Domain 3, not automatically High, in line with the written protocol. Two-gate or IC-enriched sampling was High for Domain 1. Overall risk of bias was High if Domain 1 or two or more domains were High.

Assessments were performed by a single reviewer. Dual independent QUADAS-2 with κ was not completed (protocol deviation).

### 2.8 Statistical synthesis

**Outcomes.** Pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR = Se/(1 − Sp), NLR = (1 − Se)/Sp, and DOR. The clinically relevant SROC region is low false-positive rate (FPR), matching observed studies.

**Primary model.** Bivariate random-effects model of Reitsma et al. [42], jointly modelling logit sensitivity and logit FPR with unstructured between-study covariance Ψ. Without covariates this model is equivalent to the HSROC model of Rutter and Gatsonis [43,44]. Variance of Ψ: restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Mean vector μ: generalized least squares given Ψ. Continuity correction 0.5 was added to all four cells of studies with a zero cell. Wald 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity were obtained on the logit scale. CIs for PLR, NLR, and DOR used a parametric bootstrap (10,000 draws from N(μ, vcov μ)).

The protocol named R `mada::reitsma()`. R was not available in the analysis runtime. The model was implemented in Python (`analysis/dta_meta_analysis.py`) with NumPy/SciPy. The likelihood is the same intercept-only Reitsma REML likelihood.

**Head-to-head FIA vs ELISA versus an independent reference** required ≥4 paired studies. That analysis was not fitted (k = 0 independent paired sets; k = 2 ELISA-as-reference concordance studies).

**Publication bias.** Deeks’ regression of ln(DOR) on 1/√ESS, where ESS = 4 × n_pos × n_neg / (n_pos + n_neg); two-sided *p* < 0.10 indicated possible small-study effects [45]. With k = 5 the test is underpowered and is interpreted as inconclusive rather than as proof of no bias.

### 2.9 Heterogeneity and subgroups

Heterogeneity: forest and SROC inspection; Cochran’s Q (α = 0.10) [48]; I² (0–40% might not be important; 30–60% moderate; 50–90% substantial) [48]; bivariate τ²; ρ < −0.3 suggesting a threshold effect.

Subgroup bivariate models required ≥4 studies per stratum. No stratum other than the overall model and one exploratory three-study subset met that rule. Pre-specified fever-day, infection-status, and serotype models were therefore not fitted.

### 2.10 Sensitivity analyses

Pre-specified and completed where k allowed: leave-one-out; continuity corrections 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0; 0.5 applied to all cells; STANDARD F only; reference other than NS1 ELISA alone; studies with documented fever ≤7 days (narrative if k < 3). HSROC area under the curve integrated over FPR 0–1 was **not** used as a primary accuracy measure: with k = 5 the between-study correlation was estimated at the boundary (ρ ≈ −0.97), producing a globally implausible SROC. Inference is based on the summary operating point.

### 2.11 Certainty of evidence

GRADE for DTA [49] was applied to sensitivity and specificity. Starting from high certainty for accuracy studies, rating down was considered for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness (mixed reference standards; incomplete screening), imprecision, and publication bias.

### 2.12 Protocol deviations (complete list)

1. Search date 19 August 2026 rather than September 2026 (investigator override).
2. Scopus not searched (no institutional licence).
3. ScienceDirect query condensed to the vendor Boolean limit.
4. Google Scholar: 100 of 200 protocol-cap results harvested; primary fully quoted query returned 0 hits.
5. Dual independent screening of all unique bibliographic records not completed; FIA-term filter plus scoping papers used instead.
6. Dual independent extraction and QUADAS-2 not completed (single reviewer).
7. PROSPERO CRD identifier not yet assigned.
8. Studies without documented fever ≤7 days included if they enrolled clinically suspected acute dengue.
9. Analysis engine: Python Reitsma REML rather than R `mada`.
10. Head-to-head bivariate FIA vs ELISA and all k ≥ 4 subgroups not fitted (insufficient studies).
11. Full-range HSROC AUC not interpreted (boundary correlation).

### 3.3 Risk of bias

Single-reviewer QUADAS-2 judgments are in Table 5 and Figures 2–3.

### 3.4 Diagnostic accuracy

#### 3.4.1 FIA versus study-defined reference (primary model)

The bivariate REML model included five studies and 794 specimens. Continuity correction 0.5 was applied to Zuroidah 2022 (FP = 0) and Pohekar 2017 (FN = 0).

| Measure | Estimate | 95% CI |
|---------|----------|--------|
| Sensitivity | **86.7%** | 76.0–93.1% |
| Specificity | **94.0%** | 89.6–96.6% |
| PLR | **14.3** | 7.5–26.5 |
| NLR | **0.14** | 0.07–0.26 |
| DOR | **101** | 30–333 |

Heterogeneity: Q for logit-sensitivity = 8.19 (df 4, *p* = 0.085); I²_Se = **51%** (moderate); τ²_Se (univariate DL) = 0.19. Q for logit-specificity = 6.17 (df 4, *p* = 0.19); I²_Sp = **35%**; τ²_Sp = 0.13. Bivariate τ on the logit scale: 0.64 (sensitivity) and 0.47 (FPR).

A PLR of 14.3 produces a large increase in post-test probability of acute dengue when FIA is positive [50]. An NLR of 0.14 produces only a moderate decrease; it is above the conventional 0.1 threshold for a strong rule-out test [50].

#### 3.4.2 FIA versus ELISA (paired, independent reference)

No included study tested both FIA and NS1 ELISA against an independent molecular reference on the same specimens and published both 2×2 tables. Head-to-head bivariate comparison was therefore not performed.

Two studies used NS1 ELISA as the reference for FIA (concordance, not independent accuracy): Ghogre 2021 Se 96.6% / Sp 96.4%; Pohekar 2017 Se 100% / Sp 98.4% [26,52]. These results indicate that QUANTI CARD closely tracks same-generation NS1 ELISA in those samples. They **inflate** apparent FIA accuracy relative to RT-PCR because the same analyte is measured by two immunoassays (incorporation). They do not establish interchangeability of FIA and ELISA for surveillance case confirmation.

Chaiyo et al. compared FIA and visual ICT against a composite RT-qPCR + antibody ELISA standard (not NS1 ELISA): FIA NS1 Se 79.1% / Sp 92.3% versus ICT NS1 Se 76.6% / Sp 92.3% [25]. That is a FIA versus ICT contrast, not FIA versus NS1 ELISA.

#### 3.4.3 Exploratory subset: STANDARD F against non-ELISA-only references

The three STANDARD F studies are exactly the three studies that did not use NS1 ELISA as the sole reference (Chaiyo, Zapata-de la Cruz, Zuroidah). Exploratory bivariate pooling (k = 3, below the protocol threshold of 4; interpret with caution):

- Sensitivity **80.5%** (75.1–85.0)
- Specificity **91.5%** (87.7–94.2)

This operating point is closer to published visual NS1 ICT meta-analysis sensitivity (about 71%) than the five-study primary model, because the two QUANTI CARD versus ELISA studies no longer pull sensitivity upward [29].

Documented fever ≤7 days (Chaiyo, Zapata-de la Cruz only; k = 2, not pooled): Se 79.1% and 84.4%; Sp 92.3% and 88.8%.

### 3.5 Subgroups and meta-regression

No pre-specified subgroup had ≥4 studies. Fever day 0–3 vs 4–7, primary vs secondary infection, serotype, and region were not modelled. Univariable bivariate meta-regression was not interpreted: with k = 5, likelihood-ratio tests on categorical covariates are anti-conservative.

### 3.6 Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Deeks’ test: slope 39.9 (SE 32.2), t = 1.24, **p = 0.30**. There is no statistically significant small-study effect at α = 0.10, but k = 5 has very low power [45]. The result is **inconclusive**.

### 3.8 Principal numerical findings

In five commercial FIA evaluations (794 specimens) identified from a 19 August 2026 search of 1,889 bibliographic records, FIA for dengue NS1 showed pooled sensitivity 86.7% (76.0–93.1) and specificity 94.0% (89.6–96.6), with PLR 14.3 and NLR 0.14. When analysis was restricted to STANDARD F against RT-PCR or composite standards (k = 3), sensitivity was 80.5% and specificity 91.5%. Concordance with NS1 ELISA (k = 2) was high but does not estimate accuracy versus molecular diagnosis. Heterogeneity of sensitivity was moderate. Three of five studies were at high risk of bias. Dual independent screening of the ELISA-dominant unique set was not done. Certainty is very low for sensitivity and low for specificity.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings in context

This review is, to our knowledge, the first DTA synthesis to isolate commercial reader-based FIA for dengue NS1 rather than pooling it with visual ICT. The five-study summary point (Se 86.7%, Sp 94.0%, PLR 14.3) looks, at first glance, as if FIA closes much of the gap between visual NS1 ICT (Haider et al.: Se 71.0%, Sp 94.7%) and NS1 ELISA [18,29]. That reading is too optimistic.

Two of five studies judged FIA against NS1 ELISA. Their near-perfect sensitivity is what one expects when two antigen immunoassays are compared with each other, especially when kits are supplied by the same manufacturer [52]. Removing those studies drops pooled sensitivity to 80.5% — still above Haider’s visual ICT point estimate, but with a confidence interval (75.1–85.0) that does not exclude modest superiority and does not support replacing ELISA.

Chaiyo et al. provide the fairest within-study ICT comparison: on the same 404 plasmas, STANDARD F NS1 was only 2.5 percentage points more sensitive than visual ICT, with identical specificity [25]. Zapata-de la Cruz et al. found STANDARD F NS1 versus RT-PCR sensitivity 84.4% and specificity 88.8% in a hyperendemic Mexican sample collected on fever days 2–6, likely enriched for secondary infection [53]. Versus a RT-PCR/IgM construct, FIA sensitivity fell to 57.3% because IgM-positive, RNA-negative illness is outside the NS1-only detection window [53]. That contrast is clinically important: NS1 FIA cannot be asked to detect the same case definition as a combined molecular–serological algorithm.

A PLR of 14.3 supports **rule-in** in triage when prior probability is already moderate to high, as in outbreak clinics [50]. An NLR of 0.14 does **not** allow a negative FIA to stop a dengue work-up, particularly after day 3, in secondary infection, or when warning signs are present [2,12,14].

### 4.2 Clinical and operational implications

WHO 2009 dengue guidance ties risk stratification to the period around defervescence, typically days 3–7 [2]. ELISA batching plus transport commonly yields 24–48 hours to an actionable result [15,22]. FIA in 5–15 minutes changes *when* a positive NS1 result can inform fluid management and admission, not only *whether* the assay is analytically accurate [23,25].

**Proposed placement (given these data, not as a WHO recommendation):**

1. **Days 0–3, suspected dengue, setting with a maintained reader:** FIA NS1 as a same-encounter rule-in test. Positive → dengue-protocol monitoring; confirm with ELISA or RT-PCR when capacity allows (surveillance and serotyping).
2. **Days 4–7 or suspected secondary infection:** FIA NS1 plus IgM (and IgG if immune status is needed). Negative dual testing → RT-PCR or paired serology; do not exclude dengue on NS1 alone [28,29].
3. **Severe dengue, ICU, or public-health confirmation:** RT-PCR and/or NS1 ELISA remain the reference. FIA has not been shown to replace molecular diagnosis or serotype identification [20,53].
4. **Negative FIA, high clinical probability:** never used as a stop test.

FIA still requires electricity or a charged analyzer, a functioning reader, and a cartridge supply chain. It is **near-point-of-care**, not a zero-infrastructure test. Relative to ELISA it is more deployable; relative to visual ICT it is less so. National programs should treat FIA as a **separate procurement class** from visual ICT.

Pohekar and Bhalchandra (2023) reported an inverse relationship between FIA COI and platelet count [27]. That observation is hypothesis-generating for severity correlation and was not meta-analyzed here.

### 4.3 Strengths

The review asked a technology-specific question rather than pooling “rapid dengue tests.” Identification used four bibliographic databases plus supplementary Cochrane, Scholar, and grey sources, with PRISMA-S documentation of the ScienceDirect Boolean limit and the Scopus gap. 2×2 cells were reconstructed only when published Se, Sp, PPV, and NPV identified a unique integer solution. The bivariate Reitsma model respects the sensitivity–specificity trade-off. QUADAS-2 was tailored to NS1 kinetics and incorporation bias. Protocol deviations are listed rather than hidden.

### 4.4 Limitations

**Screening.** Dual independent screening of 1,082 unique records was not done. FIA papers that do not use FIA language in the title can be missed; Zapata-de la Cruz 2026 is the example that was recovered because STANDARD F appears in the full text [53]. Ghogre 2021 and Pohekar 2017 were not in the four-database unique set and entered via protocol scoping, which is a form of citation-informed identification, not a systematic screen of the entire ELISA literature.

**Small k and mixed truth.** Five studies cannot support the protocol’s subgroup programme. Reference standards range from RT-PCR to same-analyte ELISA. ELISA-as-truth inflates agreement; RT-PCR after the viremic window misclassifies some true dengue as negative and can lower apparent FIA sensitivity [9,20]. Latent-class models were not fitted.

**Spectrum bias.** IC-cluster sampling [25] and two-gate DVI/non-DVI or ICT+/ICT− sampling [52,55] produce spectrum that does not match consecutive febrile patients. Specificity in particular can be overestimated when controls are clearly non-dengue rather than other acute febrile illnesses, including flavivirus co-circulation [17,54].

**Fever window.** Three of five studies did not report day of illness. NS1 is not constant across days 0–7 [9,12]. Mixed timing biases pooled sensitivity toward an average of two biological states.

**Industry involvement.** Pohekar 2017 used manufacturer-supplied FIA and ELISA kits [52]. Funding was often unreported.

**Single reviewer.** Extraction and QUADAS-2 were not dual-adjudicated. Transcription error in reconstructed cells is possible, although published PPV/NPV were used as cross-checks.

**Software.** The Reitsma REML fit was implemented in Python because R/`mada` was not installed. The likelihood matches the published model; numerical estimates can differ slightly from `mada::reitsma` on the same cells.

**PROSPERO.** Absence of a CRD number at submission time is a reporting weakness. The written protocol is archived in the repository.

**Pohekar 2023.** The largest single FIA versus ELISA cohort (n = 300) could not be pooled [27]. If off-diagonal cells were unfavorable, the primary pooled sensitivity would fall; if they were favorable, it would rise. Until those cells are published, the 86.7% figure is incomplete with respect to the known literature.

### 4.5 Future research

1. Prospective, consecutive, STARD-compliant studies of FIA and NS1 ELISA on the same acute specimens, with RT-PCR and serotype as an independent reference, and day of illness recorded to the day [36,51].
2. Publication of the Pohekar 2023 concordance table.
3. Secondary-infection strata with a pre-specified immune-status definition large enough for precise sensitivity differences.
4. Flavivirus challenge panels (Zika, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis) from the intended-use region [17,54].
5. Impact studies: time-to-decision, admission, and severe-dengue progression when FIA is added to usual care — accuracy alone does not establish clinical utility.
6. Dual independent screening of the 1,082 unique records remaining in this project, to test whether additional FIA reports were missed.

### 4.6 Conclusion

Early NS1 detection during the first week of fever is central to dengue care. ELISA’s accuracy is offset by infrastructure and delay; visual ICT’s speed is offset by modest and operator-dependent sensitivity [18,29]. Commercial FIA occupies the gap between those poles.

Across five studies and 794 specimens, FIA for dengue NS1 had pooled sensitivity 86.7% (76.0–93.1) and specificity 94.0% (89.6–96.6), with PLR 14.3 and NLR 0.14. The more conservative STANDARD F estimate against molecular or composite standards is sensitivity 80.5% and specificity 91.5%. Certainty is very low to low. Three of five studies were at high risk of bias, dual screening of the full unique set was not completed, and a 300-patient FIA–ELISA cohort remains unpooled for lack of a 2×2 table.

**Actionable statements that the data support:**

1. Use FIA as a rapid, objective NS1 **rule-in** test in triage and outbreak settings when a reader can be maintained — particularly in the early febrile window.
2. Do not use a negative FIA to exclude dengue; reflex to IgM/IgG, ELISA, and/or RT-PCR.
3. Do not equate FIA with visual ICT or with ELISA in guidelines or tenders.
4. Keep ELISA and RT-PCR as the laboratory reference for confirmation, surveillance, and serotype-dependent public health action.
5. Commission paired, day-stratified, serotype-aware studies with independent molecular reference standards, and complete dual screening of the already-retrieved unique bibliographic set, before making FIA the sole confirmatory test in national algorithms.

Until that work is done, FIA is a **clinically useful near-point-of-care NS1 assay** that can shorten time-to-a-positive-result without replacing laboratory confirmation.
